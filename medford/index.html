Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Medford

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, test the relief valve once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on descaling every year if their water is hard.

The age of your heater matters just as much. With regular maintenance, a tank water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts showing warning signs, like discolored hot water, popping noises, or moisture under the tank, it's smart to start planning a replacement. A planned replacement is cheaper and causes far less stress than an emergency swap after a leak.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to excess pressure or a missing expansion tank, both modest fixes. The same drip ignored for months can keep stressing the tank until a seam gives way, and a small repair becomes a full replacement and a cleanup. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and cold showers.

Hot Water Headaches Medford Homeowners Face

The majority of water heater problems we see in Medford start with what's in the water. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, minerals can be a real factor: calcium and magnesium settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the rumbling and popping you may hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling is worth keeping up with in hard-water areas.

Then there's age. Once the protective anode rod is gone, usually somewhere in the second half of a tank's life, the steel starts to rust from the inside, and a slow leak at the base is usually the first visible sign. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to tripped high-limit switches and dead elements.

Code is another piece. Replacing an older heater frequently means bringing the install up to current code: an expansion tank if the plumbing is a closed system, a T&P discharge line piped to an approved spot, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, plus straps and a pan where required. Well water brings its own challenges, such as heavier sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that sometimes shows up when certain water chemistry reacts with a standard anode rod. All of these can be fixed, and most cost far less to address early. If something seems off, a diagnostic visit from our team catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one.
